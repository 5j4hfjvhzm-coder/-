## 4. Description

> Ever tried to tickle yourself? It doesn't work, and your brain is the reason. 🧠
>
> Every time you move, your brain predicts what that movement will feel like. The cerebellum plays a key role in that prediction. When the touch matches what was predicted, your brain turns the sensation down, so it feels weaker and less surprising. When someone else tickles you, your brain can't predict the touch as easily, so it feels much stronger.
>
> Research: Blakemore, Wolpert & Frith, "Central cancellation of self-produced tickle sensation", *Nature Neuroscience* (1998); Blakemore, Wolpert & Frith, "Why can't you tickle yourself?", *NeuroReport* (2000).

## 5. Fact-check notes

- **The claim:** your own touch feels weaker because the brain predicts it from the movement command; this is called "sensory attenuation". Brain imaging found less activity in the touch-sensing (somatosensory) cortex for self-made touch. It also linked the cerebellum to predicting the sensory results of your own movements. — Blakemore, Wolpert & Frith (1998, *Nature Neuroscience*) and the 2000 *NeuroReport* review. I'm confident about the authors, the journals and the main finding. Please check the exact citations on PubMed before you publish.
- **Visual accuracy:** the touch signal in the arm nerve looks the same whether you touch yourself or someone else does. The difference is shown in the brain's response, which is weak for self-touch and strong for the other person's touch. This matches the research: the dampening happens in the brain, not in the skin's nerves.
- **Stylized parts:** the glowing "prediction" arc from the cerebellum is a motion-graphic way to show a prediction. It is not a drawing of a real nerve pathway.
